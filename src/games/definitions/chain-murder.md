## How to Play

A murder mystery travels down a chain of players through pantomime and gibberish. Each player must work out the location, the victim's occupation, and an unlikely murder weapon, then pass those details to the next player. At the end, the referee reveals how much of the original information survived.

1. **Get the secrets.** Send all but one player out of the room, where they cannot see or hear the suggestions. The remaining player is the first cluegiver. Ask the audience for a location, an occupation identifying the victim, and an unlikely murder weapon.
2. **Start the clock.** Call in the first guesser and start a three-minute timer. This is one shared time allowance for the entire chain, rather than three minutes per pair.
3. **Pass the clues in order.** Using only pantomime and gibberish, the cluegiver communicates the **location, then occupation, then weapon**. The guesser responds physically and in gibberish; save spoken guesses for the final reveal.
4. **Make the handoff.** When the guesser believes they know the weapon, they use the imaginary object to mime killing the cluegiver. Pause the timer. The guesser becomes the next cluegiver, and the previous cluegiver steps aside.
5. **Continue the chain.** Call in the next guesser and resume the timer with the time remaining. The new cluegiver passes on what they understood, using a new approach rather than simply copying the clues they received. Repeat until every guesser has received the information or the shared timer runs out. Then stop clue-giving and move to the reveal.
6. **Reveal the answers.** Line the players up in order, from the first cluegiver to the last player who received clues. Ask the last player to name the location. If incorrect, ask the preceding player, working backward until someone gets it right or the first cluegiver reveals the original suggestion. Start again with the last player for the occupation, then repeat for the weapon.

The humor comes from the changing interpretations. Players should pass on what they genuinely believe the clues mean, even when the audience knows the information has gone astray.

### Variations

- **[Chain Shopping](/games/chain-shopping/):** A family-friendly version using a shopping location, a person or occupation, and a purchased item. The guesser gives the item as a gift, and the cluegiver swoons and faints to end the handoff.

- **Audience volunteer:** A volunteer may be the final guesser. Brief them on the clue order, gibberish, and mimed weapon handoff before they enter.
- **Lunch Break:** Replace the murder suggestions with **where you had lunch, who you had lunch with, and what was in your sandwich**. Pass those details along in that order using pantomime and gibberish, then reveal them by working backward through the players.

## Examples

The audience suggests **a laundromat**, **a dentist**, and **a rubber duck**.

The first cluegiver establishes the laundromat by loading an imaginary washing machine, adding coins, and reacting to the spin cycle. They move to the occupation by inviting the guesser to examine an aching tooth. Finally, they squeeze and play with an imaginary rubber duck. The guesser uses the duck to mime an absurd murder, ending the handoff.

The next cluegiver might establish the laundromat by sorting laundry and waiting for a dryer, finding a fresh way to communicate the same location. If they misunderstood the dentist as a barber, they pass on that interpretation in good faith. The reverse-order reveal lets the audience see where the information changed.

## Player Tips

- Remember **LOW: Location, Occupation, Weapon**. Finish communicating one element before moving to the next.
- Build a small interaction around the clue. To suggest a dentist, offer a toothache that invites your partner to examine you, rather than only demonstrating dental tools at them.
- Give clear physical feedback when the guesser is on the right track. If their interpretation is wrong, respond to it and offer a different clue.
- As the new cluegiver, find fresh actions for the ideas you understood. The audience has already seen the previous approach.
- Keep gibberish free of recognizable words that reveal the answer. Let gestures, reactions, and the shared scene carry the meaning.
- Make the murder a playful mime with an imaginary object, and choose a controlled reaction that suits the space and your partner.

The scene-based clues, clear feedback, and fresh approaches above draw on [David Charles's Chain Murder Endowment guide](https://improvdr.com/2023/09/08/game-library-chain-murder-endowment/). The shared three-minute clock and reveal sequence follow the Improv Circus format supplied here.

## Host Tips

- Write down the three original suggestions and keep the clue order clear for the audience and players.
- Ask for an occupation and an object unrelated to the location so each clue adds something distinct. [Cameron D's short-form teaching notes](https://camerond.net/short_form_course.html) use this approach when gathering suggestions.
- Pause the clock between pairs and resume it when the next guesser enters; never reset it for a new pair.
- Keep waiting guessers out of earshot and ask the audience to avoid shouting the answers during clue-giving.
- At the reveal, work backward separately for each element. Give the last player the first attempt at all three answers.
- If time expires mid-pair, stop the action and move to the reveal with the players who received clues.
